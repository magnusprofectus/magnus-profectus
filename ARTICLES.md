slug: 27-seconds-why-the-rest-is-exact
title: 27 seconds: why the rest is that exact
date: 2026-10-01
blurb: Long enough to breathe. Too short to recover. The whole method lives in that gap.
---
The rest between partial sets is 27 seconds, just under 30, to leave room for racking the weight and getting set again. The exact number isn't magic, but the *idea* of it is the engine of the program: rest long enough that you can perform, not so long that you've recovered.

Here's what happens in that window. The quick energy your muscles use for hard efforts, the phosphagen system, is mostly spent during a set to failure. Its refilling is one of the best-documented curves in exercise science: at around 30 seconds of rest, roughly **half** of that energy has returned, this is the **partial recovery** the whole method rides on, and the descending rep targets you see (like 6, then 4, then 3) are its visible signature, not a flaw. The research on repeat-effort performance matches it: after a short rest like this, performance drops well below what the same rest gives after a single effort, fatigue compounds across the partial sets, which is exactly the stimulus being bought.

At 27 seconds you get back just enough to do real work again, but nowhere near enough to do the same work. So the second partial set starts strong enough to move real weight, and ends deeper into failure than the first.

Stretch the rest to two or three minutes and you're doing ordinary straight sets with extra steps, each partial set starts fresh, and the deep fibers never get the compound stimulus. Cut it to ten seconds and the second set collapses to almost nothing; you've built a failure test instead of a workout.

The 27-second window is the compromise that makes three partial sets each worth doing: hard enough to count, fresh enough to move. It's also why the rep targets for later partial sets are lower, the app derives them from recovery physiology, and the descending targets you see (like 6, then 4, then 3) aren't a failure to match the first set. They're the signature of the method. Flat reps across all three would mean the rest was too long.

You can change the rest per exercise, the app derives the rep targets from whatever rest you choose, so longer rests flatten the descent and shorter ones steepen it. For advanced users this is a real experiment space: longer rests trade stimulus-per-minute for more reps per partial set, shorter rests compress everything harder. If you experiment, change one exercise at a time and judge by the trend, not by how it feels. If you don't have a reason, don't touch the default. The number was chosen the way good training numbers always are: long enough to work, short enough to hurt.

title: Cardio: intervals on a track
date: 2026-10-14
blurb: We like cardio, as its own sport, not a weight-loss tool. Six rounds, nine minutes, twice a week.
---
We like cardio. But we keep it in its own box: **cardio is not weight lifting, and it's not weight loss either.** Cardio *can* help lose weight, but it's not the efficient path, a deficit made of fasted running mostly succeeds in making you hungrier, which is a motivation problem disguised as a metabolism problem. The fasting tiers do the deficit job without the appetite tax. (This is the same separation Dorian Yates prescribed: weights for the growth stimulus, cardio for its own variable, never letting one tax the recovery of the other.)

**What cardio is for, in our view, is a different axis of health: endurance, oxygenation, cardiovascular capacity.** And the *felt* benefit is genuinely different from being big and strong. Strength feels like capability, carrying, moving, lifting. Cardio fitness feels like lightness, breathing stays easy, stairs are silent, running for a bus or uphill or after children doesn't collapse you. It's a different flavor of *feeling healthy and vibrant*, and the longevity literature keeps finding cardiorespiratory fitness among the strongest predictors of all-cause mortality there are. Big and strong is one currency; not being out of breath at 40 is another. We'd rather hold both.

**Our preferred method: high-intensity interval running**, short sprints, short recoveries, on a loop. The research case is lopsided enough to summarize with numbers: across the big meta-analyses, interval training beats continuous moderate training for VO₂max improvement, the flagship dose-response analysis (69 RCTs) found intervals reach 80% of their maximal VO₂max effect in **~11 minutes per session** where continuous training needs ~52 minutes for the same result, a 4.6× time saving. And this isn't fringe physiology: when you examine how elite distance runners actually train, the majority of their *intensity* comes as interval sessions, the polarized/pyramidal distributions that dominate elite endurance training put most volume at easy intensity and the decisive work at very high intensity. Long-distance runners train intervals because that's what raises the ceiling that their long runs draw from.

**Why it fits our house style.** A sprint for your life is failure training for the cardio engine, the same "recruit everything by ending near the limit" logic that drives the lifting program, with the same anti-sandbagging property: at 30-second sprint intensity there is no gray zone where you're "kind of trying." You're either sprinting or you've stopped. And it's measured output: six rounds is the number, the track is the yardstick.

**The setup.** Run on a **track**, the predictable surface matters more than it sounds: no sprained ankles from forest roots, no asphalt-pounding joint cost, no traffic lights interrupting round four. People say tracks are boring; at sprint intensity you will never once have time to be bored, you'll be too absorbed surviving the round.

**The session.**
- **Warm-up thoroughly:** one or two easy jogging laps, then running drills, high knees, butt kicks, A-skips (the skip with the knee drive), carioca/grapevine sideways, and a few jumping bounds, followed by two or three short strides at accelerating speed. Then rest a few minutes, sip water, and get ready.
- **The work:** set your Gymboss, yes, the same timer as the gym, to alternating **60 seconds of slow movement (a jog) and 30 seconds of sprint for your life.** That's one round. **The goal is six rounds.** Total work: nine minutes plus warm-up.
- **The progression ladder**, expect the first two weeks to be humbling. Coming back after a long break, three rounds might look like: jog–sprint, jog–sprint, walk–sprint (the jog decays visibly). The following weeks: three good rounds, then a walk-sprint round, then a stand-and-gasp-sprint round. Eventually: **six rounds of sprinting with jogging in between**, and that, we'd argue, is very strong cardiovascular health. At that point you'll also amaze your steady-state jogging friends on longer runs, because your ceiling has risen under their floor.

**Dose and trade-offs.** Maximum **twice a week**. It's exhausting in a way people find off-putting, same objection as the lifting program, and our answer is the same: results are the motivating factor, plus the time. Nine hard minutes beat hours of weekly jogging on the fitness axis. And we suspect, though the evidence is softer here, that the repetitive-strain cost to knees and hips is *lower* than long weekly asphalt mileage, far fewer total impacts, on a more forgiving surface.

**A note on shoes.** We train, lifting and intervals, in barefoot-style shoes and have had fewer injuries since the switch, with the fair caveats: it demands an adaptation period of weeks-to-months and a change in jogging style (shorter, quicker steps, landing softer and more mid-foot). Don't switch cold and don't sprint maximal in week one of the transition.

slug: dumbbells-the-real-sweet-spot
title: Dumbbells: the real sweet spot
date: 2026-09-27
blurb: Free weights, maximum stabilisation, and safe at failure. The default recommendation, and why.
---
This program's default recommendation is **free weights, with dumbbells as the sweet spot**. Machines remain a good tool where the situation calls for them. The order of preference is dumbbells first, other free weights close behind, machines where they serve you.

Here's the reasoning from the last rep backwards.

Dumbbells are still free weights: each side of your body works independently, and the stabilisation demand is real, at the end of a hard set, the weight wobbles, and your body answers by recruiting everything available. That extra recruitment is a feature, not a bug, for a program built on near-failure work. Barbells offer some of this but lock you into a fixed bar path; dumbbells track your own geometry, which is kinder to the joints session after session.

Then there is safety at failure, which matters more here than in most programs because failure is guaranteed, three times per exercise. A failed dumbbell rep ends with the weights set down or dropped to the side. A failed barbell rep can end with the bar on you. Knowing the worst case is "I set it down" is what allows total commitment to the last rep, and total commitment is the entire stimulus.

The practical costs of dumbbells are modest and worth naming: weight jumps come in fixed steps (round the suggested warm-up and pick the nearest available), and very heavy dumbbells get awkward to mount. Machines cover both weaknesses, no stabilisation requirement at all if you're chasing pure isolation, and easy small jumps, which is exactly why they stay second-choice rather than banned. For some movements (leg curls, calf raises, certain rows) a machine is simply the right tool. Use it.

The one habit to keep: log which equipment you used and stay consistent within an exercise. The trend math compares sessions; comparing a dumbbell press to a machine press muddies it.

## Choosing exercises that fit the method

Not every exercise fits rest-pause cleanly, and two patterns are worth avoiding when you have a choice.

**Skip heavily compound movements.** A big multi-joint lift loads several muscles with one weight, and they rarely need the same relative load: the muscle you are targeting reaches failure long after a smaller helper has already given out, or the other way around. The set then measures a compromise instead of a target. Heavy compounds also carry more technique and injury risk exactly where the method guarantees failure, three times per exercise. Isolation-friendly selections keep the stimulus where you aim it and keep the last rep safe.

**Beware exercises worked one side at a time.** A one-arm row or a single-leg press breaks the rest arithmetic: while side one rests, side two works, so each side waits far longer than 27 seconds between partials, and the partial sets stop stacking fatigue the way the method needs. If equipment leaves you no alternative (the Founder's Dumbbell Program lives with this deliberately), run the sides back to back and simply breathe for 5 to 10 seconds between sides instead of using the rest-pause timer. Each side then gets a real rest pause of its own, just a slightly longer one, and the log stays honest. Use such exercises sparingly, as an equipment compromise rather than a first choice.

Every gym sees it every January: the newcomer who arrives on fire. Five sessions a week, every session maximal, a new supplement every week, equipment bought before the third workout. The fire burns hot and short, and by March the gym is quiet again. **Motivation fatigue is real, and doing too much too early is the most reliable way to earn it.**

This program's structure already protects you more than most, two to four sessions a week, an hour or less each, is a complete program, not a compromise, but the *temptation* to escalate is worth addressing directly, because it arrives wearing the costume of virtue. More looks like dedication. It isn't. **The muscle grows between sessions, not during them; the habit grows during them.** And the habit is the scarce resource: the lifters who transform over years are almost never the most intense ones in month one, they're the ones still showing up in year five.

**Our recommendation: throttle deliberately.**
- **Months 1–6: two, at most three workouts per week.** (The two-split runs perfectly at two, each split once weekly; three sessions just alternates which split gets the second hit.) Write this in stone even when, especially when, you feel like more.
- **Only after the routine has survived months**, you no longer negotiate with yourself about going, the sessions feel like part of the week's shape rather than a project, consider a third. A fourth only if life genuinely has room for it and recovery is demonstrably keeping up (the trend line will tell you: stalled numbers across the board are the signature of under-recovery).
- **For busy lives, serious careers, small children, real travel, two or three is not a stepping stone; it's the destination.** The "two workouts, forty minutes" article is our own compressed variant for exactly this crowd. Consistency at two beats ambition at five, every time it's measured.

**Recovery is where the throttling earns its keep.** Training to failure three partial sets at a time is a real stress; done hard and often, the first thing to degrade is *motivation itself*, before the numbers stall, you'll notice the dread creeping in. That's not weakness, that's recovery debt. The classic overtraining progression: enthusiasm → escalation → grind → injury or burnout → months off. Throttling the front end of that curve is how you never meet its tail.

A note taken from the source material of this program and echoed by the science: brief, hard, infrequent training is not a beginner's compromise, it's what advanced, evidence-based programming converges on as well (higher intensity needs *more* recovery, not less). You're not doing less than the program requires by training twice a week. **You're doing exactly what the program requires.** The extra sessions, the extra supplements, the extra equipment, none of it is on the requirement list. The log doesn't reward enthusiasm. It rewards showing up, beating one number, and coming back.


## ARTICLE
slug: lose-the-fat-first
title: Lose the fat first
date: 2026-10-12
blurb: One goal at a time: why aggressive weight loss and serious lifting don't mix, and a tiered fasting approach to get to your starting line.
---
If you want to build muscle but have significant fat to lose first, our advice is unambiguous: **lose the fat first, then start the program.** Not both at once.

**Why not lift while aggressively losing?** A fast-moving deficit is a terrible environment for strength work: recovery is poor, sessions feel heavy, progression stalls, and stalled sessions, logged truthfully, are demotivating. Trying to chase two goals at once usually means doing both badly and quitting both. Do one at a time. (No, you don't need to add cardio either at this stage, the deficit does the work; cardio is for health and enjoyment, not as the weight-loss engine.) Once you're down near your target body-fat level, you start this program on a **slight caloric surplus with sufficient protein**, and it does what it does.

**How to lose: fasting, tiered by speed.** Fasting works for a simple, well-measured reason, it removes calories without requiring constant willpower at every meal, and trials of time-restricted eating (8-hour windows, no calorie counting) consistently show meaningful weight loss driven by reduced intake, in the 12-month randomized trial, the 8-hour group cut roughly **425 kcal/day** and lost about 4.6 kg, essentially matching traditional calorie counting without counting anything. Choose your tier by how fast you want to lose; each tier adds to the previous one:

- **Tier 1, remove the invisible calories.** Stop drinking calories: water, black coffee, plain tea only. No juices, no milks, no sugary coffees, no sodas, including zero-calorie ones, to be on the safe side (sweeteners are debated for insulin effects; we'd rather not argue with it). And remove all snacks between the three major meals. For many people this alone is a 300–500 kcal/day drop.
- **Tier 2, daily fasting window.** 16/8 (eat within 8 hours) is the easy entry; **18/6** for significant improvement. Eat normal meals inside the window, two or three of them, not a feast compressed into hours.
- **Tier 3, add longer fasts.** The choice within this tier is purely about speed: **24-hour fasts once or twice per week** (dinner to dinner works well) is the moderate setting, skipping a day at a normal 2,000–3,000 kcal maintenance removes that day's intake entirely, and two such fasts a week cut 4,000–6,000 kcal weekly on top of the daily window. **48- and 72-hour fasts once or twice per month** are the aggressive setting, a 48-hour fast removes ≈4,000–6,000 kcal in one act, a 72-hour ≈6,000–9,000. The classic trials back the math: sustained deficits without calorie counting produced ~5 kg (5–6% of body weight) lost over 12 weeks with lean mass preserved, and chamber studies show fat oxidation climbing steadily through a fast (still rising past the 50-hour mark), so what you lose is disproportionately fat. These are advanced tools, not the entry point.
- **Tier 4, do the eating windows as keto.** Keep the fasting structure but make the meals themselves ketogenic: very low carbohydrate, adequate protein, fat filling the rest. Two well-measured effects stack on top of the fasting deficit. First, **appetite**: the meta-analysis on ketogenic diets finds hunger *falls* rather than rising during weight loss, ketosis appears to suppress appetite directly, which is the opposite of what conventional dieting does. In intake terms that's realistically another **300–500 kcal/day** of voluntary reduction. Second, the diet is self-enforcing: with carbs gone, the appetite-triggering glucose rollercoaster is structurally removed, not just managed. Keto also carries a fast **water drop**, each gram of glycogen holds ~3 g of water, and emptying glycogen releases 1–2 kg almost immediately. That's scale drama, not fat, and it comes back with the first refeed, know it before it alarms you.

The rule that makes the tiers work: **the eating windows contain normal meals, not compensation.** A 24-hour fast followed by a 2,500-calorie eating window is theater. This is also why the long fasts deliver more than their raw arithmetic: the same trials that track intake show little or no compensatory overeating on the following days, fast-day deficits mostly *stay* deficits. And the hunger story improves with time, in the eating windows too: with insulin spikes gone quiet, the glucose crashes that drive cravings flatten out, so the meals you do eat satisfy more and chase less.

**The part nobody expects: it gets easier fast.** Hunger and cravings are largely driven by blood-glucose falls following insulin spikes, every snack and sweet drink buys the next crash, and the modern eating pattern is a rollercoaster of them. Introduce fasting and the ride flattens: insulin responses settle, glucose stays stable, and within a week or two most people report cravings dropping dramatically. The 12-month trial data backs the tolerance story, people genuinely live this way for a year without suffering through it. Hunger still visits; it stops being an emergency.

**A rough worked example, all tiers stacked.** Take a common scenario: no liquid calories and no snacks, an 18/6 keto window on regular days, one 24-hour fast per week, except in the two weeks of the month that carry a 48-hour fast, which take that week's place. With a maintenance intake somewhere around 2,200–3,000 kcal/day, the arithmetic lands at roughly a **7,000–8,500 kcal weekly deficit, call it 4–5 kg of fat per month**, plus the 1–2 kg water drop in the first two weeks. Treat that number the way it's offered: as a *very* rough ceiling, not a promise. Real-world results come in below the arithmetic, water fluctuations, eating-window creep, and the body's adaptive responses all erode it, and the deficit shrinks as you shrink (a lighter body burns less). But even at half that rate, the scale would move by double digits across a season, which is why the aggressive tier exists at all.

**A note on candor:** the tiers are tools, not moral grades. Tier 2 held for six months beats Tier 4 attempted for three weeks and abandoned. Get to your target body-fat level, then come back and start the program, and the "when real life gets in the way" article applies just as much to a diet as to training: one goal, held steadily, wins.

slug: rest-pause-properly-executed
title: Rest-pause, properly executed
date: 2026-10-23
blurb: Explosive up, slow controlled down. Why the tempo matters, why the rep range is a target and never a ceiling, and why the near-failure reps are the ones that count.
---
The mechanics of this program fit in a sentence, but executing the sentence well has four moving parts. This article is the checklist.

**1. Tempo: explosive up, slow controlled down.** Lift the weight as fast as the weight allows, not jerky, but *intent on moving it quickly*, and lower it slowly under full control, two to three seconds down. The asymmetry is deliberate, on both ends:

- **The explosive concentric** recruits high-threshold motor units, the biggest fibers, which respond to *force requirement*, not just effort. Grinding a rep up slowly lets lighter fibers cover the work; moving it fast forces the strong ones to show up. It also has a psychological function: an intent-fast rep reads as "I own this weight," which is exactly the state of mind the next 27 seconds needs.
- **The slow eccentric** is where much of the growth stimulus lives, muscles produce more force eccentrically and incur more of the mechanical tension that drives adaptation there. It also keeps you in command of the descent: at the end of a partial set, a dumped negative is how form injuries happen. Control it to the bottom, reset, explode again.

**2. Control the variables, all of them.** Same weight for all three partial sets (change it next session, never mid-set). Same rest: 27 seconds, timed, not "felt about right." Same full range of motion on every rep, the last rep of the third partial set should travel the same path as the first rep of the first. Same body position: no bridging, swinging, or heaving the setup into better leverage as fatigue rises. The reason is data integrity as much as physiology: **the trend only means something if every session's numbers were earned under the same conditions.** A descending-reps chart is a diagnostic; a chart polluted by variable technique is noise.

**3. The rep range is a target, not a ceiling.** "5–7, 3–5, 2–4" tells you *where to aim* when picking the weight, it calibrates the load. It is not a quota. If you get 8 on the first partial set, you were too strong for the weight: log the 8, and go heavier next session. If you get 9, log the 9. The instruction is always **as many clean reps as possible**, every partial set, to real failure, the range exists so the *weight selection* lands you in productive territory, and the total-reps rule (past the cap, the extra reps don't count toward the progression trigger) exists so "more reps" can't substitute forever for "more weight." The failure article is the companion read here: failure, not the target, ends a partial set.

**4. The near-failure reps are the product, everything else is scaffolding.** This deserves the emphasis repeated until it's boring, because it's the concept the whole program hangs on. In a conventional set, only the last rep or two approach true failure; the preceding reps are warm-up-grade work. Physiologically, the fibers are recruited in order of need, and the strongest, most growth-responsive fibers only enter when everything before them has been exhausted, which means the productive portion of a conventional set is its final fraction. Rest-pause collapses the structure so that **almost every rep you do is that final fraction**: partial set 2 starts where failure stood 27 seconds ago, and partial set 3 starts from an even deeper place. Three partial sets of near-failure reps, perhaps fifteen total, do more productive work than twenty conventional sets spread over an hour. Several heavy sets taken properly to failure beat many sets taken comfortably: the research agrees in its own language (recruitment scales with effort), and every experienced practitioner of this method agrees in louder language.

That's the execution standard: **fast up, slow down, same everything, always to failure, one more rep whenever it exists.** The app handles the timer, the targets, the trend. This page handles the part that lives in your body.

title: Sandbagging and self-candor
date: 2026-09-29
blurb: The progress killer nobody admits to. Why the reps you skipped count against you invisibly.
---
Sandbagging means ending a set well short of what you could do, while telling yourself it was hard. It's the most common failure mode in training, and the most invisible, because the person it fools is you.

It's rarely deliberate. Discomfort rises, the mind starts negotiating, and a set that could have reached twelve ends at nine. Research on how lifters judge their remaining reps suggests we're least accurate exactly when we're furthest from failure, which is precisely where sandbagging lives. By the time you're close enough to judge accurately, the decision has already been made.

In most programs, sandbagging costs you a rep or two. Here it costs you the whole structure, because each partial set starts where the last one stopped. Sandbag the first and the second begins from a shallower place; sandbag all three and you've done a session that logs the same as a real one while producing a fraction of the stimulus. The numbers look fine. The muscle disagrees.

There is no app feature that fixes this. The timer doesn't negotiate, the trend doesn't flatter, but neither can stop you from stopping early. What helps is a standard outside yourself, the last-rep bet is the one I use, and the willingness to log the ugly truth. A session of 6/4/2 recorded truthfully is worth more than a fantasy 10/8/6, because the trend is only useful if the inputs are real. Sandbagged numbers make the app lie to you: it will recommend weights you can't handle, and hide stagnation that's already happening.

The good news: sandbagging shrinks with practice. Every time you push one rep past where you wanted to quit, the next true rep is easier to find. The skill of the last rep is trainable like any other.

The first exposure to a movement after hours of sitting acts, physiologically, partly as its own warm-up. That effect is wasted, and often painful, when the first exposure is a maximal set.

The fix is what this app already prescribes: **movement-specific ramping**, a set of the same exercise at ~50% for higher reps, ~75% for fewer, ~90% for a handful of crisp reps. Each ramped set rehearses the exact pattern, wakes the target muscle, lubricates the joints you're about to load, and gives you a live read on how the weight feels *today* before it matters. (The "wake up the muscle" point is real: this is potentiating the nervous system and muscle spindles for the load, not just warming tissue.)

There's an injury argument that deserves its own sentence: **training to failure is the highest-risk moment in lifting, and the first working set of an un-warmed muscle is where injuries cluster.** Cold tendon + max effort + the moment where form is most likely to erode, that's the recipe. A specific, controlled warm-up makes the working set land on a prepared machine, and it makes your workouts *consistent*: session to session, the first working set is performed under comparable conditions, which is what the trend math quietly assumes. A warm-up that varies with mood and lobby temperature adds noise to your own data.

Second-exercise note (the app's auto rule): the first exercise for each muscle group gets warm-ups; the second doesn't need them, the muscle is already hot. Exceptions you can toggle yourself with the WARM-UP checkbox: coming into an exercise cold after a long gap, or an injury history that wants extra ramping.
