---
title: "The arithmetic of one more rep"
date: 2026-10-16
blurb: "One rep sounds like nothing. It isn't, here's what adding a single rep per session does in percentage terms, and how to think about weight jumps."
---

The program asks you to always strive for at least one more rep, and to go up in weight at the top of the range. That can feel trivially small, one rep?, so let's put real numbers on it.

**What one rep is worth, in percentage terms, depends entirely on where you start.** The classic strength-science approximation treats reps and load as exchangeable: each additional rep at a given weight is worth roughly 2.5–3% of one-rep-max. Now do the arithmetic session by session:

- If last session's total was **12 reps** (say 6/4/2), one more rep total is **+8%** work. Two more is +17%.
- At **13 reps**, one more is **+7.7%**; two more, +15%.
- At **14 reps**, the top of the standard range, one more is **+7%**, and it's the cue that the *weight* should go up next session rather than the reps.

Read that again: **one single rep is a 7–8% jump in total work.** For comparison, the textbook recommendation for *adding weight* is 2–10% when it's time. So one rep at the same weight is not a rounding error, it's a bigger relative jump than the typical weight increase. Done every session it compounds ferociously: a modest one-rep average per session across three sessions a week is a doubled workload in a couple of months. That's the engine of this program, and it's why "just one more" is the whole game.

**When the weight goes up, compare percentages, not loads.** Here's a subtlety most trackers get wrong. When you increase the weight, your total reps usually *drops*, that's expected, not regression. If you jump from 60 kg × 14 reps to 65 kg × 11, the naive load number (weight × reps: 840 vs 715 kg) says you got 15% *weaker*. That's nonsense, you moved a bigger load per rep. The fair comparison is the **percentage weight increase for that specific workout**: +8.3% weight at 75% of the previous per-rep volume is real progress, and the next sessions at the new weight rebuild the reps from there. The app's capped-load trend handles this by comparing like with like over time rather than punishing the drop, but in your head, judge weight-jump sessions by the percentage on the plates, not by the product.

**And the size-strength nuance cuts both ways.** Reps climbing at the same weight grows the muscle; weight climbing at fewer reps grows strength and, at sufficient effort, the muscle too. This program deliberately sits in the middle: the rep range forces you to be strong *and* the total target forces enough volume to grow. (The dedicated "Size vs. strength" article goes deeper.)

**So how fast is 7% a session, really?** Compounding is the clearest frame. Add roughly one rep per session, three sessions a week, and your weekly work grows ~20%, which compounds the way money does. A few landmarks: doubling your total workload takes about **four weeks** of steady one-rep gains; a year of it multiplies your capacity by well over a hundred-fold on paper, and since nobody compounds forever (recovery, the natural limit, and the fact that heavier weights reset the rep count), the real-world shape is: *fast, visible progress for months*, then the curve flattens exactly as you approach your ceiling. That flattening isn't failure; it's arrival. Beginners see jumps every session; trained lifters earn monthly steps; both are the same 7% seen at different altitudes. The takeaway for motivation: early progress isn't a fluke you should distrust, it's the steepest, steepest part of the curve, and it's front-loaded on purpose.

Practical rules distilled: **chase one rep every session; treat it as the 7–8% raise that it is; when the top of the range is hit, raise the weight by the smallest sensible step and judge that session by plate-percentage, not total load; never let the total *drop* below last session's at the *same* weight**, same weight, fewer reps is the only true regression, and it's what the stagnation counter counts.
