The mechanics of this program fit in a sentence, but executing the sentence well has four moving parts. This article is the checklist.

**1. Tempo: explosive up, slow controlled down.** Lift the weight as fast as the weight allows, not jerky, but *intent on moving it quickly*, and lower it slowly under full control, two to three seconds down. The asymmetry is deliberate, on both ends:

- **The explosive concentric** recruits high-threshold motor units, the biggest fibers, which respond to *force requirement*, not just effort. Grinding a rep up slowly lets lighter fibers cover the work; moving it fast forces the strong ones to show up. It also has a psychological function: an intent-fast rep reads as "I own this weight," which is exactly the state of mind the next 27 seconds needs.
- **The slow eccentric** is where much of the growth stimulus lives, muscles produce more force eccentrically and incur more of the mechanical tension that drives adaptation there. It also keeps you in command of the descent: at the end of a partial set, a dumped negative is how form injuries happen. Control it to the bottom, reset, explode again.

**2. Control the variables, all of them.** Same weight for all three partial sets (change it next session, never mid-set). Same rest: 27 seconds, timed, not "felt about right." Same full range of motion on every rep, the last rep of the third partial set should travel the same path as the first rep of the first. Same body position: no bridging, swinging, or heaving the setup into better leverage as fatigue rises. The reason is data integrity as much as physiology: **the trend only means something if every session's numbers were earned under the same conditions.** A descending-reps chart is a diagnostic; a chart polluted by variable technique is noise.

**3. The rep range is a target, not a ceiling.** "5–7, 3–5, 2–4" tells you *where to aim* when picking the weight, it calibrates the load. It is not a quota. If you get 8 on the first partial set, you were too strong for the weight: log the 8, and go heavier next session. If you get 9, log the 9. The instruction is always **as many clean reps as possible**, every partial set, to real failure, the range exists so the *weight selection* lands you in productive territory, and the total-reps rule (past the cap, the extra reps don't count toward the progression trigger) exists so "more reps" can't substitute forever for "more weight." The failure article is the companion read here: failure, not the target, ends a partial set.

**4. The near-failure reps are the product, everything else is scaffolding.** This deserves the emphasis repeated until it's boring, because it's the concept the whole program hangs on. In a conventional set, only the last rep or two approach true failure; the preceding reps are warm-up-grade work. Physiologically, the fibers are recruited in order of need, and the strongest, most growth-responsive fibers only enter when everything before them has been exhausted, which means the productive portion of a conventional set is its final fraction. Rest-pause collapses the structure so that **almost every rep you do is that final fraction**: partial set 2 starts where failure stood 27 seconds ago, and partial set 3 starts from an even deeper place. Three partial sets of near-failure reps, perhaps fifteen total, do more productive work than twenty conventional sets spread over an hour. Several heavy sets taken properly to failure beat many sets taken comfortably: the research agrees in its own language (recruitment scales with effort), and every experienced practitioner of this method agrees in louder language.

That's the execution standard: **fast up, slow down, same everything, always to failure, one more rep whenever it exists.** The app handles the timer, the targets, the trend. This page handles the part that lives in your body.
