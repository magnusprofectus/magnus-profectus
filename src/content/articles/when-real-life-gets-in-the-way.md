title: "When real life gets in the way"
date: 2026-10-08

---

Real life always gets in the way eventually. An injury. An illness. A work assignment that eats a quarter. A new child who eats a year. Every training program is written for the weeks when nothing happens; the question that decides whether you'll still be training in five years is what happens in the weeks when something does.

This program has an unusually good answer, and it comes from how it's built.

**The log is outside your head.** Most people's training lives in their memory, what they lifted, how it felt, where they were. A break scatters that: you come back uncertain, guessing at weights, quietly embarrassed. Here, everything is stored: every exercise, every session, every trend. Your program survives the break even when you didn't.

**Starting over at half is normal, not shameful.** The app doesn't compare you to your old self, it compares you to last session. If you return after months away and start at 50% of your previous working weight, the progression rules treat that exactly as they treat any starting point: do the work, beat the number. The first few sessions feel humbling; the trend line doesn't care, and within a few months you're typically back at your previous best, because of muscle memory.

**The biology is genuinely on your side.** Muscle fibers acquire extra nuclei during training, and those nuclei are retained through long periods of detraining, the cellular basis of "muscle memory." Studies in both animals and humans show that previously trained muscle regrows substantially faster than untrained muscle, and that strength re-acquires in weeks, not months. A related mechanism is neurological: the skill of the lifts, coordination, motor learning, never fully leaves, which is why returning lifters feel "back" within a handful of sessions even before the muscle has fully returned. (The science is nuanced, some studies find the retraining advantage smaller than the folklore claims, but the practical experience of every returning lifter is consistent: regaining is far faster than building was.)

**The sessions themselves are hard to lose.** A program that needs five gym nights a week collapses at the first busy month. Two sessions a week, 40 minutes, from a corner of your home: travel means packing less than a pair of shoes; a sick child means moving the session a day, not losing the week; a work crunch means the program waits patiently instead of punishing you.

One rule from experience: when coming back after a long break, start *lighter* than you think you need, the first sessions after a break are for re-grooving the movement, not for proving something. Then let the progression do what it does. It doesn't care that you're starting low. Neither should you. The limit is still where it was, and months, not years, will get you back there.
