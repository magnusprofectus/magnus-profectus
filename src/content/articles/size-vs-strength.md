"Size vs. strength" is a false war fought by people using different weapons. Here's what the research actually says, where this program sits, and why the deciding evidence should be your own log.

**The research, in three lines:**

1. **For strength:** heavier is better, decisively. Meta-analyses comparing load schemes (sets held equal) consistently favor high loads for 1RM gains, the 2017 Schoenfeld review found a moderate-to-large advantage for loads above ~60% of one-rep max, and later analyses put the effect around ES 0.4–0.6. The "strength zone" (1–5 reps at 80%+) wins on maximal force.

2. **For size:** effort is the variable, not the load. The same meta-analytic literature, surprisingly to most people, finds **similar hypertrophy across a huge load range (≥30% 1RM), provided sets go to failure.** Light weight to failure and heavy weight to failure grow about the same muscle. The catch: light-load sets need many more reps, take longer, and hurt more (high metabolic acidosis), which is why moderate loads win on *efficiency and adherence*, not biology.

3. **For both at once:** mixed loading shows no special magic, and heavy-load-plus-high-volume raises joint stress and recovery costs for no proven size dividend.

**So where does rest-pause sit?** Squarely in the efficient middle, and here's why its mechanics make that so:

- **Rep range ~5–7 at the first partial set** places the working weight around 75–80% of 1RM, the heavy side of the hypertrophy zone, close enough to the strength zone to drive load-dependent neural adaptations (high-threshold motor units get recruited by load *and* by effort).
- **Failure is mandatory**, which is the variable that equalizes hypertrophy across loads, so the size benefit of the light/lighter schemes is captured without their time cost and discomfort.
- **Partial sets 2 and 3 start at failure's doorstep**, meaning nearly every rep performed is a near-maximal-effort rep, the condition under which even light loads grow muscle maximally, here combined with heavy loads.
- **Volume per exercise is small**, avoiding the heavy-load-plus-high-volume recovery trap.

In other words: the method takes the strength advantage of heavy loads, the size independence of training to failure, and compresses both into a two-minute working set. That's why it produces both outcomes at once where the textbook continuum says you must choose, the continuum assumes *conventional sets*, with only the last rep near failure; rest-pause makes every rep qualify.

**Now the caveats.** All of the above is theory fitted to studies, and science, for all its value, tests narrow questions: eight weeks, one load scheme, one muscle, trained or untrained college students. It cannot test *your* physiology, *your* schedule, *your* plateaus, or what years of a method feel like. Neither the studies nor any author's recommendations outrank direct personal experience, which is exactly why the anecdotal reports in "what the friends said" exist, and why this program is **free to try with this app**: no paywall before the verdict.

So the closing implore, deliberately stated: **run it for a couple of months and be astounded at the results.** Two to four hour-or-shorter sessions a week, one number to beat per exercise. Whatever the theory says, the trend line of your own log is the experiment that actually applies to you, and it's the one no study can run for you.
