# The {PROTOCOL_NAME} Guide

## You're putting in the work. So why isn't it showing?

You train. You show up, do your sets, go home. Yet month after month, the mirror and the numbers barely move.

Most people conclude the problem is genetics, age, or not doing enough: more sets, more days, a new program. Usually it's none of those.

## Most of your reps don't count

Muscle grows in response to hard work, and specifically to the last few reps of a set: the ones where the weight slows down and your body has to recruit every fibre it has to move it. Coaches and researchers often call these *effective reps*. The reps before them are, for growth purposes, mostly warm-up.

Now picture a typical set of 10. Stop with three or four reps still in the tank and maybe one or two of those reps did the real work. Multiply that across a whole workout and you spend an hour in the gym for a few minutes of actual stimulus.

## Sandbagging: the progress killer nobody admits to

**Sandbagging** means ending a set well short of what you could actually do, while telling yourself it was hard.

It's rarely deliberate. Discomfort rises, the mind starts negotiating, and a set that could have reached 12 ends at 9. Research on how well lifters judge their remaining reps suggests we're least accurate exactly when we're furthest from failure, which is where sandbagging lives.

It's also invisible. Nothing measures it. You leave feeling you worked hard, and nothing tells you otherwise. Repeat that for months and you get the result most gym-goers have: effort without progress.

## What it costs

Three sets, six exercises, three sessions a week: that's more than 2,800 sets a year. If most of them stop short, most of your training time isn't building anything.

That's not a motivation problem. It's a design problem, and design problems can be fixed.

## A better way: the {PROTOCOL_NAME}

Pick a weight you can lift about 7 times. Lift it to failure. Rest 27 seconds. Lift it to failure again. Rest 27 seconds. Once more. Done.

A typical set: **6 reps… 4 reps… 3 reps = 13 reps** with a weight you could normally lift only 6 times.

**Why it works:**

- **Heavy weight *and* high reps.** Normally you choose: heavy for strength, lighter for size. Rest-pause gives you both: the load of a heavy set with the total reps of a muscle-building set.
- **Almost every rep counts.** After the first partial set, every partial set starts close to failure, so the reps that follow are the hard, productive ones.
- **Sandbagging becomes impossible.** You don't decide when a partial set ends; failure does, three times in a row, and a 27-second clock doesn't negotiate.
- **Short, focused sessions.** One all-out set per exercise, six exercises per session, done in about an hour.
- **Proof every session.** Each exercise produces one number: total load moved. Within seconds you know whether you got better.
- **Backed by evidence.** In controlled studies, rest-pause training has matched or outperformed traditional sets for strength and muscle growth, usually in less time.

## How to train

### Your first session with an exercise
Choose a weight you believe you can lift for about **7 good reps**. Getting it slightly wrong is fine; the app will steer you over the next sessions.

### Warm up the first exercise for each muscle group
Three quick sets: about **50% × 15 reps, 75% × 7, 90% × 3**. Crisp, easy reps. Warm-ups prepare you; they shouldn't tire you. Take **1–2 minutes** between warm-up sets, then a **longer break (2–4 minutes)** before the working set so it starts fresh. The second exercise for the same muscle doesn't need warm-ups. Suggested warm-up weights are rounded **down** to the nearest whole kg/lb, just pick the nearest weight that exercise actually has.

> **In a hurry?** The app's **Warm-up circuit** (Train screen) lists every warm-up set of the session on one page, rotated across exercises, so the whole warm-up is done in one pass. See the "Saving time" article in the Extra tab.

### The working set
- **Same weight for all partial sets.** Adjust next session, never mid-set.
- **Every partial set: as many reps as you can possibly do.** Not "as many as the target says", **as many as your body can physically produce** before failure. The targets below are a weight-selection guide, not a stopping point; see the failure section below.
- **The targets:** 5–7 reps, then 3–5, then 2–4, **12–15 in total.** These ranges tell you whether the *weight* is right. If you stop at 5 because 5 is in the range, you have not done a partial set, you have done a warm-up rep at working weight. Hit 7? If the bet test (below) says another rep exists, keep going.
- **Rest 27 seconds** between partial sets. That's just under 30, to allow for racking the weight and restarting. Start the timer the moment the weight is down.
- **Failure means the last rep you can complete with good form**, not a twisting, grinding half-rep. When form breaks, the partial set is over.

### Failure is not a suggestion. It is the entire program.

Everything in this method rests on one idea: **the reps closest to failure are the only ones that matter.** Muscle fibers are recruited in order of need, the easy reps at the start of a set use only the fibers needed for an easy task. As fatigue builds, your body is forced to call up more and more fibers, and the very last fibers recruited, the strongest, most growth-prone ones, never get touched unless you push into true failure. That's why this program works with so few sets: every partial set begins at failure's doorstep, so nearly every rep you do recruits near-maximal fiber. Three all-in partial sets here do more for muscle growth than ten comfortable ones.

That's also why **sandbagging**, stopping because it burned, or the clock beeped, or you "felt done", quietly kills the program. The last rep before failure feels exactly like the rep that IS failure. Your body cannot tell them apart. Your mind will always tell you to stop one rep early. So you need a standard that isn't a feeling.

Here it is:

> **Imagine you had to bet everything you own on the answer, house, savings, the lot. The bet: "I cannot do one more clean rep." Would you take that bet? If you would not, if any chance remains that another rep is there, you have not reached failure. Anything before that point was just discomfort.**

When the weight is down and your lungs are burning, ask the bet question. Would you put everything you own on "one more is impossible"? If you wouldn't, one more is possible, grind it out. When you genuinely would take that bet, you've reached failure: write the number down and rest 27 seconds, because the next one starts from there. Did you try and the bar simply stopped? That's failure too, same thing, write it down.
- **Lower under control, lift with intent.** No bouncing, no swinging.

### Progress
- **15 total reps? Go heavier next session.** Reps beyond 15 don't count toward your load, because the weight has become too light.
- **Under 12? Go lighter.** The weight is too heavy for this protocol.
- Remember: the 12–15 total is how the app judges the *weight*, not how many reps you're allowed. Within any single partial set, the rule never changes, **as many as possible, to failure.** If your totals land at 13 because you stopped early, the app will think the weight is perfect while you're quietly doing nothing for growth.
- **After a weight increase your load may dip.** If you still reach 12 reps, that's progress, marked ★. Build reps at the new weight from there.
- **Rate each set 1–3** (1 = easy, 2 = right, 3 = too heavy). It's a note to your future self about what to expect.

### When to switch exercises
If an exercise hasn't beaten your recent best for two sessions in a row, swap it for another one for the same muscle. That isn't failure. The exercise has done its job.

### The standard week
The main version of this program is a **2-split**: two workouts, upper body and lower body, **six exercises per workout**, done **two to four times a week**. Two sessions is a complete program; three or four is faster progress if recovery allows. With the prescribed warm-ups and rests, a six-exercise session runs **about an hour**, sometimes a little more.

### Between exercises and between sessions
Rest **2–4 minutes** between exercises so each one starts fresh, and expect the session to take about an hour. To speed the session up, shorten rests *between exercises* first, never between partial sets.

Growth happens outside the gym: sleep, enough protein and total food, and rest days. Every 6–12 weeks, consider an easier week and mark it as a **deload** so it doesn't count against your progress.

## Custom exercises

The built-in exercise library covers the standard movements, but **adding your own is often easier**, many exercises are variations with names your gym uses, and the app doesn't care whether an exercise is a library one or yours. When adding an exercise to a workout, choose **Create custom exercise**: a name and muscle group is all that's required. Under "More options" you can set rest seconds and a first-partial-set target, from which the app derives the rest of the rep targets, leave it alone and sensible defaults apply. Custom exercises behave identically to library ones: full history, trends, stagnation tracking, renaming, warm-up control.

## Safety

- **Technique first.** Only use exercises you can perform well. If in doubt, learn them from a qualified coach or a good tutorial before using them here.
- **Barbell lifts to failure** (bench press, squat, presses) need safety arms, pins or a spotter. Machines and dumbbells are the safest choices for this protocol.
- **Pain is information.** Muscle burn is expected; sharp or joint pain is not. Stop, flag **PAIN**, and if it returns next time, switch the exercise.
- **Bodyweight exercises** (pull-ups, dips, push-ups) can be tracked, but they're not ideal here. Near failure, technique erodes and momentum creeps in. Prefer machines, cables and free weights where you can.

## Entering weights

Enter weights the way you think about them, and stay consistent for each exercise:

- **Dumbbells:** the weight of one dumbbell (two 25 kg dumbbells → enter 25).
- **Barbells:** total weight, total plates, or plates per side. Any of these works, as long as you stick to it.

Two optional fields make warm-up recommendations accurate:

- **Base weight:** anything that's there before you add weight. The bar (e.g. 20 kg), a machine sled, or your bodyweight for bodyweight exercises.
- **Per side:** switch on if you enter the weight added to *each* side.

*Example:* a 20 kg bar with 20 kg on each side. Enter **20**, set base weight to **20**, turn **per side** on. The app knows the real load is 60 kg.

## Nutrition

Nutrition is a deep topic of its own and far beyond the scope of this guide. A few pragmatic notes for recovery and progress, not medical advice:

- **Protein with every meal.** Aim for at least **20 g of animal-based protein** per meal as a floor, eggs, meat, fish, dairy.
- **Around training.** A **protein isolate** shake during or after workouts is an easy way to cover the day's needs.
- **Creatine.** Daily **creatine monohydrate** (3–5 g) is well studied and a reasonable extra boost for strength and recovery.
- **Not while cutting.** We do not recommend this high-intensity method when trying to lose weight. Lose the weight first, e.g. with intermittent fasting, LCHF or keto, then run this program in a **protein-rich caloric surplus** to build.
